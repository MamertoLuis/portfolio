The salt had worked its way into Arthur’s skin decades ago, settling into the creases around his eyes and the stiffness in his knuckles. Every evening followed the same unhurried rhythm: climb the spiral iron stairs, trim the wick, polish the Fresnel lens until the glass sang under his cloth, and watch the beam carve an arc through the Atlantic dark.

He had heard the old sailors’ tales of what lurked beyond the surf—ghost ships, sea monsters, and maidens with silver tails. He dismissed them as the delirium of men drunk on rum and solitude. 

Until the night of the autumn squall.

The gale hit with teeth, tearing spray off the breakers and throwing it against the lantern room glass. Out past the reef, where the current churned into white foam, Arthur saw a flash of movement—not the sleek grey of a seal, but a long, iridescent tail that shimmered emerald and mother-of-pearl under the sweeping light.

He grabbed his lantern and slicker, descending to the base of the tower. The tide was surging violently over the rocks. Wedged between two jagged granite teeth in the tidal pool below lay a figure, pinned beneath a tangle of heavy storm-tossed driftwood and kelp.

Arthur scrambled across the slick stones, fighting the wind. When he hauled the timber away, he found himself looking into eyes the color of deep ocean trenches. Her hair was like dark sea-grass, her skin pale as sea foam, fading below the waist into gleaming, scaled fin. She bared needle-sharp teeth, hissing in terror and pain; a jagged gash ran along her flank.

"Easy, girl," Arthur murmured, keeping his voice low, the way he spoke to frightened gulls stranded on the ledge. "I’m not here to harm you."

He didn't try to pull her from the water. Instead, he fetched clean gauze, fresh water, and iodine from the keeper’s quarters. Kneeling by the surge pool, he washed away the grit and pressed the dressing to her side. At his touch, her shivering slowed. She tilted her head, watching the wrinkled hands work with steady, quiet care. 

When the tide rose high enough to flood the pool, she pushed gently against the rocks. For a moment, she hovered at the surface, her webbed hand lightly brushing the frayed cuff of his coat, before diving into the dark, rolling swell.

The storm passed by morning. Arthur returned to his duties, though the silence of the tower felt heavier now.

Two nights later, the wind fell calm, leaving the sea like black glass. A soft, rhythmic splashing drew Arthur to the iron balcony. Down below, sitting on the low rocks where the tide lapped gently, sat the creature. When the lighthouse beam swept overhead, catching her in its brilliant white path, she lifted her arm, holding aloft an unbroken, luminous conch shell.

Arthur smiled, raising his brass lantern in return. 

From that night on, the watch was no longer lonely. Whenever the fog crept in thick enough to swallow the world, Arthur would hear a low, haunting melody rising from the waves, keeping time with the turning light.
